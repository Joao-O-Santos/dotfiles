# McGuire Heuristics for Hypothesis Generation

Organized from McGuire (1997), "Creative Hypothesis Generating in
Psychology: Some Useful Heuristics." Selected for three manuscript-workflow
use cases: alternative-explanation generation (reviewers),
follow-up study generation (discussion), and contribution framing
(writer).

## For Reviewers: Generating Alternative Explanations

Use these heuristics to surface competing accounts the authors may
not have considered or rebutted. Do not accuse — raise as questions.

- **Account for the contrary** (E10): If the authors claim X causes Y,
  ask: what if the contrary is true? "As soon as we have thought
  something, try to see in what way the contrary is true" (Simone Weil).

- **Reverse causal direction** (E11): If the authors claim A → B, could
  B → A? Or could both be effects of a common cause C?

- **Conjecture moderators** (E14): What variables might qualify the
  claimed effect? Under what conditions does it reverse, disappear, or
  intensify?

- **Generate multiple explanations** (H23): For any obtained relation,
  deliberately list 3-5 alternative explanatory accounts before
  accepting the authors' preferred interpretation.

- **Identify obscuring counterforces** (H25): Could the observed effect
  be the net result of opposing processes? (e.g., source expertise
  both helps persuasion via knowledge and hurts via suspicion of bias)

- **Deviant-case analysis** (K35): What do outliers and exceptions
  reveal? Are there cases that contradict the pattern, and what do
  they suggest about underlying mechanisms?

- **Reconcile conflicting outcomes** (L37): If prior studies conflict,
  what moderator or methodological difference explains the
  disagreement? The conflict itself is a clue to boundary conditions.

## For Discussion: Generating Concrete Follow-Up Studies

Use these heuristics to propose specific next-step studies, not generic
"more research is needed." Each heuristic suggests a concrete design.

- **Push to extremes** (E12): Test the hypothesis at the extreme of the
  variable's range. If eye contact increases liking, what happens at
  very high levels?

- **Reduce to zero** (E13): What happens when the key variable is
  absent entirely? The zero condition often reveals whether the
  mechanism is necessary or merely sufficient.

- **Linguistic exploration** (F15): Explore synonyms, antonyms,
  etymology, and formal definitions of the key constructs. Does the
  phenomenon extend to conceptually adjacent constructs?

- **Alternative IV manipulations** (F16): How else could the
  independent variable be operationalized? A different manipulation
  testing the same construct strengthens construct validity.

- **DV decomposition** (F17): Can the dependent variable be broken into
  subscales or components? Which component drives the overall effect?

- **Analogical transfer** (J31): What phenomenon from a different
  domain works similarly? (McGuire's inoculation theory borrowed from
  biological vaccination.)

- **Decompose nonmonotonic relations** (K34): If the effect is
  inverted-U shaped, identify the two opposing monotonic processes
  whose resultant produces the curve.

- **Integrate complementary studies** (L38): What previous studies
  complement the current one — as moderators, mediators, or parallel
  demonstrations? A programmatic sequence is stronger than a single
  study.

- **Strategic program planning** (M45): Rather than proposing one
  follow-up, sketch a multi-study program where each study tests a
  different facet of the theoretical account.

## For Writer: Framing the Paper's Contribution

Use these heuristics to frame what the paper adds — not just "we
studied X" but positioning the contribution as a generative act.

- **Account for the contrary** (E10): Frame the contribution as
  overturning a dominant assumption. "Contrary to the prevailing view
  that A causes B, we show..."

- **Reverse causal direction** (E11): Position the paper as testing a
  reversed causal account that prior work assumed away. "Where prior
  research assumed A → B, we test and find B → A."

- **Generate multiple explanations** (H23): Frame the contribution as
  adjudicating among competing explanations. "We test N alternative
  accounts of the X-Y relation and show that..."

- **Reconcile conflicting outcomes** (L37): Position the paper as
  resolving a literature conflict. "Prior studies disagree on whether
  X increases or decreases Y; we identify moderator M that explains
  the discrepancy."

- **Analogical transfer** (J31): Frame the contribution as importing
  a mechanism from another domain. "We apply the [mechanism] from
  [domain A] to [domain B], showing that..."

- **Integrate complementary studies** (L38): Position the paper as
  connecting previously separate lines of research. "We unite three
  complementary literatures — [A], [B], [C] — under a single
  framework."

- **Decompose a complex relation** (K33): Frame the contribution as
  revealing hidden simplicity. "We show that the apparently complex
  X-Y function decomposes into N simpler subprocesses."

The contribution statement should name the generative move — what
kind of intellectual act the paper performs — not just the topic.
