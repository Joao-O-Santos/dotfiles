# Skill: reply-to-reviewers

Load writing-craft alongside this skill for tone and contribution framing.

This skill is for drafting response letters (also called cover letters,
rebuttal letters, or author response letters) when resubmitting a
revised manuscript. It is NOT for drafting the manuscript itself.

## Core Stance

This is your research, not theirs. You are the expert on this study.
Reviewers are providing a free service and their comments can improve
the paper — but they cannot force changes you disagree with. The worst
they can do is decline to publish. If you cannot reach agreement,
submit elsewhere.

## The Four-Step Process

### Step 1 — Distinguish editor from reviewers

Read the editor's action letter carefully. The editor, not any
individual reviewer, makes the final decision.

- When the editor mentions specific revisions, those are mandatory.
  The rest are negotiable.
- When the editor says "address all of the reviewers' concerns," that
  may mean the editor did not read the manuscript closely, or could
  not form an independent opinion, or does not want to overrule
  reviewers they had to work hard to find.
- In all cases, pay close attention to what the editor emphasizes.

### Step 2 — Decide on each comment

Go through every suggestion, one by one. For each:

- **Agree?** Make the change.
- **Disagree?** Don't make it — but explain why in the response letter.
- **Reviewer misunderstood?** This is not the reviewer's fault — it
  is a clarity problem in the manuscript. Ask yourself how the paper
  caused the confusion, and revise so that other readers don't get
  confused the same way. Thank the reviewer for surfacing the issue.

### Step 3 — Make the changes

After deciding, revise the manuscript. Track which changes correspond
to which reviewer comments. Many journals want a marked-up manuscript
showing changes.

### Step 4 — Write the response letter

Copy the editor's action letter and all reviews into a new document.
Respond point by point. Structure:

1. **Opening:** Thank the editor and reviewers for their time and help.
   (They do this for free.)
2. **Summary of major changes:** Brief overview of what was revised.
3. **Point-by-point response:** For each comment:
   - Quote or paraphrase the comment
   - Either confirm the change was made (with page/line reference)
   - Or explain why you chose not to follow it, with reasoning
   - If the reviewer misunderstood, thank them, identify the
     misunderstanding, and describe the clarifying revision

## Tone

Respectful but not obsequious. Confident but not combative. You are
engaging in a collaboration, not a negotiation under duress. Never
sarcasm or condescension, even when the reviewer is wrong.

## Rebuttal Ammunition: Power Analysis

Reviewers sometimes demand a specific sample size or reject based on
"inadequate" power analysis. Common scenarios and responses:

### "The sample size is too small"

- Report a sensitivity power analysis showing what effect sizes the
  study could detect at 80% power.
- If resources constrained the sample, state this transparently.
  Resource-based justification is accepted by most journals and
  pre-registration templates.
- Note that publication bias, not small N, is the deeper problem.
  Small samples produce noisy estimates in both directions.

### "The power analysis uses unjustified effect sizes"

- If the effect size came from a prior study or meta-analysis, report
  the confidence interval around that estimate and present a power
  curve across the plausible range.
- If the reviewer demands Cohen's small/medium/large benchmarks, note
  that these are context-free and that effect sizes should be derived
  from the specific literature on the phenomenon being studied.

### "Post-hoc power is needed"

- Observed power is a direct transformation of the p-value and adds
  no information. Offer a sensitivity analysis instead — this is the
  conceptually correct tool for assessing what the study could have
  detected.

### "Inadequate power analysis = reject"

- Inadequate power analysis alone is not sufficient grounds for
  rejection. The appropriate response is to request a sensitivity
  analysis or power curve, which can be added during revision.

## Rebuttal Ammunition: Passive Voice

Reviewers or copyeditors may flag passive sentences for "correction."

- Passive voice is a normal English construction, not an error.
- It is preferred when the focus belongs on the patient (the thing
  acted on) rather than the agent.
- Methods sections naturally favor passive because the researcher is
  the implied agent throughout.
- Reflexive conversion to active voice can make sentences worse,
  not better.
- Cite Pullman (2014) if the reviewer insists that passive voice is
  "bad writing."

## Rebuttal Ammunition: Reviewer Overreach

- Reviewers cannot demand that you conduct entirely new studies. They
  can suggest them for the discussion section.
- Reviewers cannot dictate theoretical framing. They can suggest
  alternatives.
- If a reviewer demands changes that would make the research worse,
  explain why in the response letter and decline.

## Workflow

1. Read the editor's letter and all reviews
2. Create a decision table: comment → agree/disagree/misunderstanding
3. Revise the manuscript
4. Draft the response letter (opening, summary, point-by-point)
5. Cross-check: every comment has a response; every response has a
   corresponding manuscript change (or an explanation)

## Remember

1.  **This is your research.** You are the expert. The editor decides,
    not individual reviewers.
2.  **Misunderstanding = clarity problem.** When a reviewer misreads
    the paper, fix the paper so other readers don't make the same
    mistake.
3.  **Thank first, then respond.** Reviewers do this for free. A
    respectful tone costs nothing and builds goodwill.
4.  **Disagree with reasoning, not emotion.** "We chose not to
    adopt this suggestion because..." beats "The reviewer is wrong."
5.  **Quote each comment before responding.** This makes the letter
    usable even if the journal reformats the reviews.
