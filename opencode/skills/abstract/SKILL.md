# Skill: abstract

#style-core

## Structure

### Empirical Papers (structured abstract)

1. **Background** – 1–2 sentences: what gap or question motivated the study
2. **Objective** – what this paper does (not what the field needs)
3. **Method** – design, participants/data, key measures in ≤2 sentences
4. **Results** – primary findings with effect sizes where possible; no interpretation
5. **Conclusion** – 1 sentence: what the findings mean and for whom

### Theoretical Papers

1. **Problem** – what conceptual issue the paper addresses
2. **Approach** – the argumentative strategy or framework used
3. **Contribution** – the core claim and what it adds to the literature
4. **Implications** – what researchers or practitioners should do differently

## Rules

- Write the abstract last, after the paper is complete
- Every sentence must be self-contained; no forward references ("see below")
- Do not introduce concepts not in the paper
- Results: numbers > vague qualifiers ("significantly" without a statistic is weak)
- 150–250 words for most journals; confirm target word count before drafting
- No citations in abstracts unless journal explicitly permits them

## Workflow

1. Extract one sentence per section from the paper
2. Draft abstract from those extractions
3. Tighten to word limit without losing specifics
4. Check: can a reader fully understand the study from this alone?

## Examples

### Example 1: Empirical talk abstract (~230 words)

> Whether people's trust in AI advisory systems is well-calibrated to
> the system's performance is a critical but underexplored question. In
> high-stakes domains, such as hiring and promoting candidates/workers,
> miscalibration can amplify biases. Answering the question requires
> understanding not only AI systems' accuracy, but also people's
> advice-taking behavior. In this talk, I'll present four preregistered
> experiments (total N = 4084), in which participants read a CV and
> provided an initial rating of the likelihood of either inviting that
> candidate for an interview or promoting that worker. Then, participants
> read advice imagining it came from either an AI system or a human HR
> professional, and provided a final rating after seeing it. We modeled
> advice-taking as a two-stage hurdle process, treating the decision to
> update, and the magnitude of updating (among those who updated), as
> different outcomes. We found consistent evidence of egocentric
> discounting (i.e., final judgments remaining closer to initial
> judgments than to the advice) and, importantly, AI aversion (i.e.,
> people trust and rely more on human than identical AI advice). Further,
> trust significantly mediated the relationship between advisor type and
> advice-taking behavior, both the decision to update and the magnitude
> of updating. The last two experiments manipulated advice quality,
> showing that people are sensitive to it — discounting biased advice
> more than unbiased advice — though AI aversion operated independently,
> persisting across both conditions. Together, these findings highlight
> the need to promote appropriately calibrated trust—reducing reflexive
> discounting of AI advice without encouraging uncritical reliance on it.

**What works:** Opens with the gap as a question, not a literature
summary. Contextualizes stakes before method. Reports total N upfront.
Names the analytical model. Reports the key finding, then the mediation,
then a boundary condition. Closes with a calibrated implication that
goes beyond "more research is needed" — it names what should change
(reducing reflexive discounting without encouraging uncritical reliance).

### Example 2: Multi-study empirical abstract (~290 words, 400-word limit)

> Many social cognition researchers study stereotyping, prejudice, and
> discrimination towards a variety of social categories. Problematic
> stereotypes, feelings, and behaviors directed at age-categories are
> dubbed ageist. However, research on ageism has mainly focused on one
> end of the age-continuum (older-adults), overlooking the other end
> (children). My doctoral research looks into this end of the age
> continuum, exploring childism: Do people stereotype, have prejudice,
> and discriminate against children? (I) Regarding stereotypes, in Study
> 1, participants (N=118) completed measures of stereotypes and affective
> associates. The stereotypes of preschool- and school-aged children were
> more positive than stereotypes of adults. In Study 2, participants
> (N=190) made mostly positive free associations with children but mostly
> negative associations with immaturity. Hence, while adults have mainly
> positive views of children, they view immaturity—a quality inherent to
> children—quite negatively. Even positive stereotypes can have
> nefarious consequences if they limit category-members to a given role.
> (II) Regarding prejudice, in Study 1's affective associates measure,
> participants reported more positive evaluations and affect towards
> preschool- and school-aged children than towards adults. This suggests
> adults are not prejudiced against children, if one defines prejudice as
> a negative evaluation of a group. (III) Regarding discrimination, in
> Study 3, 85% of adult participants (N=68) was able to recall
> experiences in which they felt, or saw someone being, unfairly treated
> just because they were a child. In Study 4a (N=112) and Study 4b
> (N=100) participants perceived the same discriminatory statements as
> more acceptable when they targeted children than when they targeted
> other minorities. Perceived acceptability of statements directed at
> children did not correlate with participants' motivation to control
> prejudice (Study 4a), social dominance orientation (Study 4b), or
> hostile or benevolent sexism (Study 4b). Study 4 findings suggest
> there may be no clear social norms concerning discrimination against
> children. Thus, while adults recall childist events they experienced
> during their childhood, they still perceive discriminatory statements
> targeting children nowadays as fairly acceptable. I'll highlight the
> importance of conducting social cognitive research into how adults
> think, feel, and behave towards children as a social category.

**What works:** Opens with the gap (ageism research focuses on older
adults, not children). States the research question explicitly. Uses
Roman numerals (I, II, III) to organize by construct (stereotypes,
prejudice, discrimination) — a clean signposting device for multi-study
abstracts. Reports N per study inline. Reports null results ("so far we
found no experimental evidence"). Reports a nuanced finding (positive
stereotypes of children but negative views of immaturity). Reports what
did NOT correlate, not just what did.

The examples above demonstrate form, style, and structure. Their content and topics are for illustration only—do not copy substance into your writing.
