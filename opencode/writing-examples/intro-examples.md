# Introduction — Theoretical Hook (Personal Anecdote)

**Source**: Published paper (2015). Learning to Represent Exact Numbers.
**Skill**: `intro`
**Pattern**: Anecdote → why it matters → conceptual stakes → preview

This article is about how young children acquire a number system. The question is how
children learn to represent exact, cardinal values such as three and seven and eventually, 2015.
What makes this topic interesting is that a number system is a cultural product, but one
that makes a big difference to individual cognition. When I recently asked my teenage son to
babysit his eight-year-old brother for the evening, he replied that for $5 per hour, he would
promise to stay in the house, and to call an ambulance in case of emergency. To actually play
with his brother, he wanted $7.50. If he was also expected to make dinner for his brother and put
him to bed, that would be $10. The eight-year-old then interrupted, offering to make his own
dinner and put himself to bed for the bargain price of $7.50.

It's hard to imagine this conversation occurring without benefit of a number system, as
all of us used numbers both in our thinking and in our communication with each other. This is
why number learning is interesting. Whereas there are many examples of children learning new
concepts (e.g., Mammals are animals that have fur and feed their babies milk), a number system
is actually a new representational resource, enabling people who acquire it to represent a whole
domain of information (i.e., information about large, exact numerosities) that would otherwise be
difficult or impossible to represent.

# Introduction — Theoretical (User's Voice)

**Source**: Unpublished manuscript. Sorting Out the Replication Crisis with Philosophy of Science.
**Skill**: `intro`
**Pattern**: Epigraph → extended analogy (economics crisis) → gap identification → structure preview

*"Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be understood. Now is
the time to understand more, so that we may fear less."* (Marie Curie)

Reminiscent of an economic crisis, the so-called replication crisis in
science (RSC) created a climate of uncertainty, provoking emotional
reactions and radical calls to action. After economic crashes some
experts may crunch the numbers looking to improve economic models, while
others may call for new regulations on the markets to deter investor
misconduct. In the case of the RSC, some critics argued for improving
statistical models (Amrhein & Greenland, 2018; Amrhein et al., 2019;
Gelman & Loken, 2013; Stark & Saltelli, 2018). Other critics made the
case for restructuring incentive structures to stop pressuring
researchers to engage in questionable research practices (QRPs, see
Simmons et al., 2011; John et al., 2012). There were also those who
developed tools to detect evidence of publication-bias, *p*-hacking or
fraud (e.g., Schimmack, 2014; Simonsohn et al., 2014; Schimmack &
Brunner, 2017).

Public debates on economic crises may neglect insights from economic
theory and related fields on how we can improve our economies. Likewise,
we believe the RSC debate largely ignored insights from the philosophy
of science. [...] Aiming to fill this gap, we
draw from foundational theories of the philosophy of science to present
recommendations for how to overcome the RSC.

We start by reviewing how the RSC unfolded [...]
Then, we draw from foundational
theories of the philosophy of science to reevaluate the RSC [...]
Finally, we continue to draw from philosophy of science to suggest old
but untried solutions for moving forward our field.

# Introduction — Thesis Opening (User's Voice)

**Source**: PhD thesis (2024). "You're Too Young to Understand": Investigating Childist Beliefs and Practices. Universidade de Lisboa.
**Skill**: `intro`
**Pattern**: Provocative questions → historical framing → plausibility → gap identification

Children should be seen not heard! You're too young to understand! No kids allowed!
Have you ever heard any of the previous statements? Have you ever felt treated unfairly during
your childhood simply for being a child? Could children be discriminated just for being children?
In more technical terms, could children be discriminated for their social category membership?
At first, this hypothesis may seem farfetched. Yet, if we look at: a) the historical treatment of
children, b) how dehumanized peoples were compared to children to legitimize their treatment
(Rollo, 2018a; 2018b; Saminaden et al., 2010), or c) the social power differential between adults
and children, the hypothesis starts seeming more plausible. Importantly, this notion has already
been proposed by scholars and practitioners working in fields other than social psychology (e.g.,
social work, Boylan & Ing, 2005; psychiatry, Westman, 1991). Yet, as far as we know, this
hypothesis has yet to be studied, in this formulation, by social psychology.

# Introduction — Review Paper with Personal Voice

**Source**: Published review paper (2023).
**Skill**: `intro` / `lit-review-section`
**Pattern**: Epigraph → personal anecdote → empirical motivation → theoretical stakes → preview

"Being human is about becoming. Sometimes we need to pause and ponder, but mostly it is our action or lack of it that determines who we are and how we become." — Emmy van Deurzen

Some years ago, my team launched a research program to empirically test whether children are regarded as less human than adults — that is, whether children are dehumanized. The idea was quite simple: Our mental representation of a prototypical human being is an adult person (e.g. [1]). Children, by definition, are not yet adults. Might this imply that we think of children as not yet fully human?

The literature hints that this might be so. For example, Black people, Native Americans, and indigenous people of South America were likened to nonhuman animals and children by White people, in the past and now [2,3]. Adults perceive infants' mental capacities to be more similar to dogs' or chimpanzees' than to their own [4]. Childlike is a marker of animalistic dehumanization when applied to social categories or individuals [5] and perceived higher competence, agency, and civility predict lower dehumanization [6–8]. Thus, empirically testing whether children are dehumanized seemed worthwhile.

[...]

As a psychologist, used to thinking about each individual, including children, as a whole person in their own right, this response to the manuscript surprised me and triggered my curiosity to better understand different perspectives on what it is to be (deemed) human. In this paper, I will focus on two different perspectives on the question "What is it to be human?", integrating literature from biology, philosophy, psychology, and children's studies. One perspective is consistent with the notion that children are human becomings and the other with the notion that children are human beings. Toward the end, I will propose that both perspectives have relevant implications to other social categories, beyond children, and will propose a reconciliation of both perspectives.

# Introduction — Theoretical/Methodological with Personal Voice

**Source**: Published theory paper (2015).
**Skill**: `intro`
**Pattern**: Strong thesis → "there are problems here" signposting → argument preview

Scientists pay lip-service to the importance of replication. It is the "coin of the scientific realm" (Loscalzo, 2012, p. 1211); "one of the central issues in any empirical science" (Schmidt, 2009, p. 90); or even the "demarcation criterion between science and nonscience" (Braude, 1979, p. 2). Similar declarations have been made about falsifiability, the "demarcation criterion" proposed by Popper in his seminal work of 1959. As we will discuss below, the concepts are closely related—and also frequently misunderstood. Nevertheless, their regular invocation suggests a widespread if vague allegiance to Popperian ideals among contemporary scientists, working from a range of different disciplines.

Experimental social psychologists have fallen in line. Perhaps in part to bolster our sense of identity with the natural sciences, we psychologists have been especially keen to talk about replication. We want to trade in the "coin" of the realm. As Billig (2013) notes, psychologists "cling fast to the belief that the route to knowledge is through the accumulation of [replicable] experimental findings" (p. 179). The connection to Popper is often made explicit.

There are problems here. First, there is the "lack" of replication. If replication is so important, why isn't it being done? This question has become a source of crisis-level anxiety among psychologists in recent years, as we explore in a later section. The anxiety is due to a disconnect: between what is seen as being necessary for scientific credibility—i.e., careful replication of findings based on precisely-stated theories—and what appears to be characteristic of the field in practice.
